Why I still hand-solder my prototypes (and when I don’t)

PUBLISHED JUN 14, 2026 READING TIME 4 MIN TAG OPINION

Ask around and you’ll hear that hot air is faster, cleaner, and what “serious” work uses. That’s mostly true. But six years of broken boards has taught me something the tutorials never say out loud: the tool that finishes the job isn’t always the tool you should reach for first. Here’s when I put the hot air station down and pick up the iron instead.

The case for hand-soldering

An iron gives you feedback the hot air simply can’t. You feel the joint wet out. You watch the flux flow. You know within a second whether a pad is bad. Hot air is a broadcast — heat goes to the target, the neighbours, the plastic connector two millimetres over, and the poor QFN on the other side of the PCB you forgot was there. An iron is a conversation with one joint.

For a prototype, feedback is the whole game. I’m not soldering a production run — I’m debugging as I build. Every joint I hand-place tells me something about the board before I ever power it on: was the pad clean, was the paste wet, did the leg move when I nudged it. That data disappears the moment I switch to hot air, where components go from “placed” to “attached” in a single indistinct puff.

When I switch to hot air

Three situations, always:

  • Removing anything. Trying to hand-remove a QFN is how you lift pads. Hot air removes it in one motion, cleanly, every time.
  • QFN and BGA installation. No exposed leads to touch means no iron can help you. Paste, place, hot air — that’s the workflow, full stop.
  • Batch work. When I’ve already prototyped the design and I’m building three more of the same board, hot air pays for itself in the first hour.

The unfashionable truth

The station you actually keep is more valuable than the one you should own.

Beginners buy hot air because it looks professional, then hand-solder anyway because the iron is faster to reach for. Meanwhile the hot air station sits on the shelf collecting dust, teaching them nothing. Buy the iron first, get very good with it, and add hot air the day you can articulate exactly what it’s solving. That day comes — but not on day one.

A note on flux

Both tools depend entirely on flux, and I still meet makers using leftover flux from an old junk box like it’s all the same stuff. Buy a real no-clean paste in a syringe. It costs less than a cable. Your joints will improve overnight and you’ll stop blaming the iron.
